About this resource

At the Child Health and Physical Activity Lab, we are passionate about helping children move more and experience the many benefits of active play. To create this Activity Bank, we turned to the experts: early childhood educators!

This resource brings together activities submitted by educators from across Canada—activities they enjoy leading and that children in their centres love to participate in. The collection includes ideas for children of different ages and abilities, with options for various group sizes, indoor and outdoor settings, seasons, durations, and equipment needs.

Use the filters below to find activities that suit the children in your care and the resources available to you. The Activity Bank can be accessed on a computer, tablet, or phone. To keep a copy of your selected activities, click “Print/Save as PDF.” Only activities matching your selected filters will be included, making it easy to create a customized collection that you can print or refer to later.

If you have any questions about the Activity Bank, please contact us at ttucker2@uwo.ca or hcoylea@uwo.ca .

How it works

Hide themed picture clues and visual markers around the classroom or outdoor space (e.g., green footprints). Children follow the trail to find a final picture of the featured character, such as the Grinch, Bunny, or Cupid.

Make it easier

Reduce the number of items and use large, visible objects with matching picture clues. Search in a smaller area, give one clue at a time, and allow children to work with a partner.

Make it more challenging

Increase the number of clues, spread them across a larger search area, or make some clues less visible. Add simple riddles, directional words, counting tasks, movement challenges, or clues that must be completed in a particular order. Older children can work in teams, create clues for one another, or search within a time limit.

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Facilitator (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Added guidance: Use picture, object, colour, tactile, or spoken clues and place items along an accessible route at varied reachable heights. Children can search with a partner, point or direct instead of collecting, or take the role of clue-giver or item checker.

Required equipment

Category: Loose parts or miscellaneous materials

Equipment and materials: Picture clues and themed visual markers (e.g., green footprints and picture of the Grinch at the end)

How it works

Facilitator creates a story using animal yoga poses (cat, cow, cobra, monkey, etc.) and children's names from the group. Each child follows doing the poses on their mat that are demonstrated by the facilitator. Warm up and cool down with belly breathing, using the stuffies on children's tummies, so that they can see them rise and fall as they breathe.

Make it easier

Spend a few days just learning the poses, and photograph children doing the poses, to post around the room (for practice, and while following the story).

Make it more challenging

Have older children anticipate the name of the pose from the demonstration, or demonstrate the pose from the name (if familiar with yoga poses).

Activity Support

How it works

Play The Floor Is Lava song and have children follow its movement directions, such as crawling, hopping, skipping, and dancing. During each countdown, children move to a designated safe surface before the 'lava' reaches the floor.

Make it easier

Place large safe surfaces close together so children can reach one within the song’s countdown. Keep the route flat and uncluttered, demonstrate each movement, and allow children to use a simpler movement such as walking, stepping, crawling, or moving with support. An adult or peer can help children identify and reach the nearest safe surface.

Make it more challenging

Place the safe surfaces farther apart or use smaller designated spaces. Encourage children to complete more complex versions of the movements in the song, such as hopping on one foot, balancing, moving sideways, or travelling around simple obstacles. Children can also try to reach a particular safe surface or share limited safe spaces cooperatively before the song’s countdown ends.

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Facilitator (2 adults); Participant (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Educators look at what works for their children individually and create an environment where everyone can participate in all activities with minimal disruption to the flow of the room

Required equipment

Category: Loose parts or miscellaneous materials

Equipment and materials: Music player and speaker

How it works

Set up a simple obstacle course using loose parts such as cones, hoops, mats, blocks, balance markers, or tunnels. Children travel through the course while moving like different animals—for example, hopping like a frog, crawling like a bear, waddling like a duck, slithering like a snake, or stretching like a giraffe. Use animal picture cards or music to introduce each movement. Children can follow the same animal movement through the entire course or choose a new animal at each station.

Make it easier

Remove the obstacles and practice one animal movement at a time in an open, uncluttered space. Alternatively, create a shorter course with only one or two wide, low obstacles. Use familiar animal picture cards, demonstrate each movement, and allow children to walk, crawl, move while seated, use a mobility device, or choose their own way to represent the animal.

Make it more challenging

Increase the materials to make it more challenging for older children, or additional instructions for example "Use one hand, one foot".

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Facilitator (1 adult); Supervisor (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Create a wide, stable obstacle-course route with enough space for mobility devices. Use low, clearly marked obstacles and provide an accessible option at each station, such as moving around, between, or alongside an obstacle instead of climbing over it. Place animal picture cards at reachable heights, demonstrate each station, and offer a peer or adult partner when needed. Children can complete the course while walking, crawling, moving while seated, using a mobility device, or directing a partner through the animal movements.

How it works

Children work individually, with a partner, or as a group to keep one or more balloons in the air without letting them touch the ground. They can tap the balloons using their hands or other agreed-upon body parts. Use balloons in a variety of colours and encourage children to count their taps or take turns keeping the balloons up.

Make it easier

Use one large, lightweight balloon in a small, uncluttered area. Allow children to use both hands or any comfortable body part, remain in one place, and let the balloon bounce once before returning it to the air. An adult or peer can help redirect the balloon toward the child.

Make it more challenging

Add more balloons, use only one hand, or ask children to tap the balloon using an agreed-upon body part, such as a foot, elbow, or head. Children can follow a colour sequence, alternate turns with a partner, move around the play space, or try to keep the balloon up for a set number of taps.

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Supervisor (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Offer balloons or lightweight alternatives in different sizes, colours, textures, and speeds. Children may participate while standing, seated, lying down, or using a mobility device and can tap, push, kick, catch, direct, or use a lightweight paddle. Use high-contrast colours, reduce the number of balloons and background distractions, and provide a peer or adult partner when helpful. Children may also count taps, choose the next colour, or direct the group’s actions.

Required equipment

Category: Portable play equipment

Equipment and materials: Balloon

Children may explore, sort, arrange, build, point, choose materials, direct a partner, or describe their creation. How it works

Hide a selection of objects or “treasures” throughout a defined indoor or outdoor play area. Give children verbal, picture, colour, or object-based clues to help them locate each item. Children walk through the space individually or with a group, follow the clues, and continue searching until all the treasures have been found.

Make it easier

Hide fewer items in a small, clearly defined area and place them where they are easy to see and reach. Use large objects with matching pictures or colours, give one clue at a time, and show children an example before the search begins. Children may search with an adult or peer partner.

Make it more challenging

Hide more objects, spread them across a larger area, or make them less visible. Use simple riddles, directional language, counting clues, categories, or clues that must be followed in sequence. Children can remember several items at once, record what they find, or create and hide treasures for another group.

Inclusion strategies: Place treasures along a wide, obstacle-free route and at heights that can be seen or reached by all children. Offer clues through pictures, high-contrast colours, objects, textures, gestures, signs, or spoken descriptions. Children may walk or move through the space, search from one accessible location, work with a partner, point or direct someone to an item, carry a checklist, give the clues, or check off the treasures as they are found.

How it works

Extend chalk or large-scale drawing into a collaborative body-tracing activity. One child chooses a comfortable position on a large sheet of paper or another safe drawing surface while a peer or adult traces around their body. Children take turns being traced and helping with the outlines. They can then decorate the tracings by adding clothing, facial features, body parts, patterns, or imaginative details. Always ask for the child’s permission before tracing their body and offer alternatives for children who do not wish to be traced.

Make it easier

An adult can complete or help with the outline while the child remains in one comfortable position. Use thick chalk, crayons, or markers that are easy to grasp, and secure the paper so it does not move. Children can decorate a prepared outline, add simple marks or colours, or trace a hand, foot, mobility aid, favourite object, or another smaller shape instead of a full body.

Make it more challenging

Invite children to create different body positions, such as stretching their arms, bending their knees, or making an action pose. Children can trace their peers with less adult assistance and add labelled body parts, clothing, patterns, or details showing what the person is doing. They can also compare poses or combine several tracings to create a larger scene.

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Facilitator (1 adult); Participant (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Place the paper on the floor, a low table, an easel, or a wall to provide an accessible working position. A child may stand or sit beside the paper, remain in a wheelchair or supportive chair, or have a hand, foot, shadow, mobility device, or chosen object traced instead of their body. Provide thick or egg-shaped chalk, triangular crayons, markers with foam grips, or tool holders. Children may trace with a peer or adult, take turns drawing different sections, decorate an existing outline, select colours, describe what to add, or direct another person’s drawing. Use a strong light to create a traceable shadow when appropriate, and check that lighting does not create visual or sensory discomfort.

Required equipment

Category: Loose parts or miscellaneous materials

Equipment and materials: Chalk, Paper

How it works

Create a pretend “crocodile river” using a foam or inflatable ball pit filled with balls or soft toys. Arrange low, stable stepping stones or purpose-made balance equipment to represent rocks leading across the river. Children move from rock to rock, check the pit for pretend crocodiles, enter it safely, and crawl out before the crocodiles can “chomp.” An adult or child may play the crocodile using gestures or pretend snapping motions without grabbing or making unexpected physical contact. Use equipment according to the manufacturer’s directions, provide close supervision, and avoid unstable stools, unsecured milk crates, or jumping into a ball pit.

Make it easier

Use fewer, wider stepping stones placed close together on a flat, non-slip surface. Remove the ball-pit entry and exit if it creates a barrier, allowing children to finish beside the pit or place a pretend crocodile into it instead. Children may walk along a marked floor path, step over flat “river rocks,” or move with support from an adult or stable handrail.

Make it more challenging

Add more stepping stones, vary their safe spacing, or create a longer route with changes in direction. Children can carry a lightweight object, pause in a balance position, follow a specific movement pattern, or choose the safest route across the river. Keep all platforms low, stable, and appropriate for the children’s abilities.

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Supervisor (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Provide a wide, stable route with low stepping stones, high-contrast or tactile markers, and enough space for mobility devices. Offer an equivalent floor-level path around or alongside the obstacles. Children can cross while walking, crawling, moving while seated, or using a mobility device, and may enter the pit using a stable transfer method only when appropriate. They may also participate by choosing the route, placing the stepping stones with assistance, checking for crocodiles, giving movement cues, or playing the crocodile from a comfortable position. Ask before offering physical assistance and avoid surprise touching or chasing.

Required equipment

Category: Portable play equipment

Equipment and materials: Balls, Stepping domes, Ballpit filled with balls or cushions/stuffed toys

How it works

After a fresh snowfall, create a network of wide paths in the snow along with a fox den and a goose house, represented by large areas of packed snow. Choose one child to be the fox while the others are geese. The geese travel along the prepared paths as the fox tries to tag them. Geese may use the goose house as a temporary safe space. When tagged, a goose moves to the fox den. Continue until all the geese have been caught, or play for a set period before choosing a new fox.

Make it easier

Create a small play area with short, wide, firmly packed paths and place the goose house close to the children. Use one fox, allow the geese to remain in the safe space as long as needed, and have everyone walk rather than run. Instead of being removed from play, tagged geese can perform a simple movement before immediately rejoining the game.

Make it more challenging

Allow free geese to enter the fox den and tag one captured goose to release them. Add more connecting paths, limit how long geese can remain in the safe house, or introduce more than one fox for a large group. Children can also travel using an agreed movement, such as hopping or moving sideways, when the snow conditions are safe.

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Facilitator (1 adult); Participant (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Make the paths wide, firm, level, and connected so children using mobility devices can participate where conditions permit. Clearly mark the fox, den, and goose house with high-contrast flags, pylons, pictures, or sound cues. Match the fox’s speed to the group and allow walking, wheeling, crawling, or another agreed movement. Use a scarf, foam wand, verbal call, or proximity tag instead of direct body contact. Children may participate with a peer partner or take roles such as fox, path guide, signal caller, den keeper, or timekeeper. Adults should not carry a child through a running game; use an accessible route or another active and meaningful role chosen with the child.

Required equipment

Category: No equipment

Equipment and materials: No equipment required

How it works

Children sit in a circle while one child, the caller, moves around the outside. The caller indicates each child in turn while saying “duck.” When the caller says “goose,” the selected child gets up and follows or chases the caller around the circle. The caller tries to reach and sit in the open space before being tagged. The selected child then becomes the next caller.

Make it easier

Use a smaller circle and have the caller and selected child walk rather than run. Clearly identify the open space and practise one round slowly with an adult modelling each role. The caller can say the selected child’s name along with “goose,” and a peer or adult can accompany either child around the circle.

Make it more challenging

Increase the size of the circle or use an agreed movement, such as hopping, skipping, side-stepping, or moving like an animal. The caller can use a quiet visual signal instead of saying “goose,” requiring children to watch carefully. Children can also move in pairs or reverse direction after an agreed signal. Avoid relying only on speed to increase the challenge.

Activity Support

Adult involvement: Supervisor (1 adult)

Inclusion strategies: Arrange the circle with enough space for supportive seating and mobility devices, and keep the route wide and free of obstacles. Replace tapping with pointing, saying a name, showing a “goose” card, ringing a gentle bell, or passing a soft object. Children may walk, wheel, move with a partner, or travel a shorter route to a clearly marked space. Offer visual and spoken cues, preview the roles, and allow children to participate as the caller without travelling around the circle if preferred. Ensure children can decline chasing or physical contact and choose another active role.

How it works

Combine soccer skills with the traditional What Time Is It, Mr. Wolf? game. One child or educator stands at one end of the play area as Mr. Wolf while the other children line up at the opposite end, each with a soccer ball. The children call, “What time is it, Mr. Wolf?” Mr. Wolf responds with a number, and the children move their balls forward by that number of dribbles or gentle kicks. When Mr. Wolf calls “Dinnertime!”, children stop their balls and return to the starting line while Mr. Wolf tries to tag someone. For safety, children should stop or leave their balls before the chase begins.

Make it easier

Reduce the distance and allow children to move the ball using any comfortable method, including kicking, rolling, pushing, or carrying it. Use large, lightweight balls and let children complete one movement for each number rather than several controlled dribbles. An adult or peer can move alongside the child, and the “Dinnertime” round can involve walking back without a chase.

Make it more challenging

Increase the distance between the starting line and Mr. Wolf, require children to use smaller or alternating-foot touches, or set up pylons for children to dribble around. Mr. Wolf can call a soccer action along with the number, such as five inside-foot touches or three passes with a partner. More experienced children can use their non-dominant foot or try to keep the ball within a marked pathway.
